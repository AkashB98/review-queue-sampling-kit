# review-queue-sampling-kit

**What this proves about Akash:** he can design the measurement layer of a human-review operation — given a frozen classifier, a queue it flagged, and a labeling budget far smaller than the queue, he can spend that budget so the classifier's true error rate comes back *unbiased, with a confidence interval*, and so a single bad segment hiding in the queue actually gets caught — and he can show exactly how the design fails when the classifier's risk score stops meaning anything.

Motivated by integrity / trust-and-safety review workflows: classifier evaluation plus human review of labeled samples, where reviewers can only ever label a fraction of the queue.

> **Synthetic data only.** Every case is generated by a deterministic seeded script. This is **not** his employer's data — no real review data, no personal data, no network, no API keys.

Stdlib-only Python 3. Zero third-party imports.

## The problem

A classifier flags 6,000 cases. Human reviewers can label **300** of them. Two questions have to be answered from those 300 labels:

1. What is the classifier's true error rate over the full queue?
2. Is one segment of the queue much worse than the rest?

How you *choose* the 300 decides whether either answer is trustworthy. Pick them naively and the bad segment gets ~32 labels — a point estimate with a confidence interval too wide to act on. Spend the budget by the classifier's own risk score, with the right estimator, and the same 300 labels answer both questions. Spend them by a risk score that has quietly stopped correlating with errors, and the fancy design does *worse* than random — unless you floor it.

## The headline (seeded queue, budget 300, truth = 0.0790 overall)

Planted truth: `standard_a` 2,000 cases at 0.032, `standard_b` 1,800 at 0.0511, `standard_c` 1,500 at 0.0213, and one planted bad segment, `new_cohort`, 700 cases at **0.4086**.

| Design | Overall estimate | SE | 95% CI | Bad-segment estimate (n) | Bad-segment 95% CI | Flagged? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Simple random | 0.0900 | 0.0165 | [0.0626, 0.1278] | 0.4375 (32) | [0.2817, 0.6067] | **No** — CI half-width 0.163 |
| Proportional stratified | 0.0833 | 0.0124 | [0.0591, 0.1076] | 0.5429 (35) | [0.3819, 0.6953] | **No** — CI half-width 0.157 |
| Risk-weighted + Horvitz–Thompson | 0.0849 | **0.0081** | [0.0691, 0.1007] | 0.4337 (70) | [0.3292, 0.5382] | **Yes** |

The flag rule is pre-declared and identical for every design: a segment is flagged when its CI lower bound sits above the design's own overall estimate **and** its CI half-width is at most 0.12 — separation plus enough precision to act on. SRS *sees* the bad segment (0.4375) but cannot certify it: 32 labels leave a ±0.16 interval. Risk-weighting concentrates labels where errors live (70 in the bad segment), halves the overall standard error, and the segment interval tightens to ±0.10 — narrow enough to flag. Across 24 fresh queues the Horvitz–Thompson estimate is approximately unbiased: mean estimate 0.0838 against mean truth 0.0841 (bias −0.0003).

## The failure case: when the risk score lies, the floor is the product

Same machinery, second scenario: the risk scores are **shuffled** across cases, so the score carries no information about errors (labels and the multiset of scores are unchanged; only the pairing is destroyed). Empirical standard errors across 24 fresh queues at budget 300:

| Scenario | SRS | Risk-weighted, no floor | Risk-weighted, `pi_floor = 0.01` |
|---|---|---|---|
| Risk score is good | 0.0152 | **0.0060** | 0.0066 |
| Risk score is shuffled | 0.0152 | **0.0429** (2.8× SRS) | 0.0214 |

Unfloored risk-weighting under a useless score is a variance bomb: an error that happens to carry a near-zero risk score enters with an enormous Horvitz–Thompson weight. The floor — `pi_i = pi_floor + (budget − N·pi_floor) · risk_i / Σrisk`, a named config knob (`--pi-floor`) — guarantees no inclusion probability below 0.01, so no weight exceeds 100. It cuts the blow-up roughly in half (0.0429 → 0.0214) and costs about 9% efficiency when the score is good (0.0060 → 0.0066). Both numbers are reported, not hidden: the floor is insurance, and insurance has a premium. (SRS is identical in both rows because it never looks at the score — that is precisely its robustness and its ceiling.)

## An honest note on the method

The methods here are textbook survey sampling — simple random and stratified estimation, probability-proportional-to-size selection, and the Horvitz–Thompson estimator are decades old; the value of this kit is the working demo, the failure case, and the discipline around them, not new methodology. Deliberately out of scope: Neyman allocation, active learning, sequential/adaptive stopping, and any reviewer-cost model — the budget is a label count, spent once, by a pre-declared design.

## How this differs from a fixed inspection sample

A sibling kit (`transaction-monitoring-rule-tuning-kit`) already contains a "below-the-line sample": a fixed every-Nth inspection of cases below a rule's threshold, with no estimator attached — it tells you what a sample *contains*, not what the queue's rate *is*. This kit does the other job: it allocates a labeling budget across the whole queue by explicit inclusion probabilities and reports an **unbiased error-rate estimate with a confidence interval**, plus per-segment estimates precise enough to flag one segment. Fixed cadence for spotting examples; probability design for measuring rates.

## Quickstart

```bash
python3 demo.py                          # end to end with asserts
python3 cli.py generate                  # the seeded queue + planted truth
python3 cli.py estimate --budget 300     # all three designs at one budget
python3 cli.py report                    # full report as JSON (sweep + study)
python3 cli.py check; echo $?            # 0 = every headline claim holds
python3 -m unittest discover -s tests    # 30 tests
python3 evals/run_evals.py               # 10 golden evals
```

`check` exit codes: **0** = every headline claim holds on the seeded data, **1** = a claim does not hold, **2** = bad input (e.g. budget below 1, or a named input file that does not exist). A budget sweep at 150 / 300 / 600 labels is in the report; the risk-weighted SE falls 0.0124 → 0.0081 → 0.0046 as the budget grows.

## How this maps to a customer POC

- **Pre-declared budget and flag rule:** the customer fixes the labeling budget and the actionability rule ("flag a segment only when its interval clears the overall rate with half-width ≤ 0.12") *before* sampling — the acceptance criteria are inputs, not post-hoc readings.
- **Design choice with the failure case priced in:** risk-weighted sampling is proposed only alongside its miscalibration study and its floor; the POC report shows what the same budget buys under SRS, so the customer sees the gain *and* the insurance premium in the same table.
- **Acceptance measurement:** the delivered number is an unbiased rate with a confidence interval and per-segment intervals — a measurement a customer can set an SLO against — not a raw count from a convenience sample.

## Architecture

| Module | Job |
|---|---|
| `generator.py` | Seeded synthetic queue: 4 segments, one planted bad segment, risk scores correlated with errors — or shuffled (miscalibrated) |
| `sampling.py` | The three samplers (SRS, proportional stratified, systematic PPS), Wilson/normal CIs, Horvitz–Thompson estimator, the `pi_floor` mechanism, the flag rule |
| `report.py` | Headline comparison at budget 300, 150/300/600 sweep, 24-queue multi-seed study (good vs shuffled risk, floor on/off), headline claims |
| `cli.py` | `generate` / `sample` / `estimate` / `report` / `check` (exit 0/1/2) / `demo` |
| `demo.py` | End to end with asserts: flag contrast, HT accuracy, blow-up and floor cap |

## Evals

`python3 evals/run_evals.py` → **10/10 golden evals**, report committed at `evals/eval_report.json`:

- **G1** planted structure: 6,000 cases, exactly one bad segment (0.4086 vs 0.0213–0.0511)
- **G2** every sampler spends exactly the budget at 150/300/600, no duplicates
- **G3** at budget 300, risk-weighted HT flags the bad segment; SRS does not
- **G4** HT approximately unbiased across 24 fresh queues (bias −0.0003)
- **G5** risk-weighted beats SRS precision, headline and multi-seed
- **G6** miscalibrated risk: unfloored HT empirical SE blows up (0.0429 vs SRS 0.0152)
- **G7** the floor caps the blow-up (0.0214) at a small good-scenario cost (0.0066 vs 0.0060)
- **G8** floor mechanism is real: min π ≥ `pi_floor`, probabilities sum to the budget
- **G9** CLI `check` exit codes 0 / 2 / 2 (pass / bad budget / missing file)
- **G10** full report deterministic; risk-weighted SE falls as budget grows

Unit tests: `python3 -m unittest discover -s tests` → **30 tests**, covering a hand-computable Horvitz–Thompson case, the floor mechanism (min π, sum-to-budget, rejection of an oversized floor), exact-budget sampling for all designs, determinism, Wilson CI behaviour, generator structure (one bad segment; shuffling destroys correlation but preserves labels and scores), the miscalibrated variance ordering, CLI exit codes, and a no-network guard (sockets blocked).

## Scope and honest limits

- **Synthetic, planted structure.** The bad segment and the risk–error correlation are built in at known strengths, so the flag contrast proves the machinery, not that a real queue's bad segment sits at 41% or that a real risk score correlates this cleanly. Real segments overlap, drift, and are rarely labelled by a single binary truth.
- **The risk score here is strong.** Errors draw risk from [0.45, 1.0] and non-errors from a near-zero-heavy distribution. A weaker real score buys less precision; the shuffled scenario is the limiting case, and a partially-informative score lands between the two study rows.
- **The flag threshold is a convention.** Half-width ≤ 0.12 is stated up front and applied identically to all designs, but it is a chosen actionability bar, not a theorem; a customer with a different bar gets a different flag table.
- **HT variance estimator.** The kit uses the standard Poisson-style HT variance estimator under systematic PPS selection; it is honest at these sample sizes but is an approximation to the exact without-replacement variance.
- **One-shot, uniform-cost labels.** Every label costs one unit of budget and the design is fixed before sampling. Reviewer time actually varies by case difficulty, and adaptive designs exist — both are named as out of scope rather than silently assumed away.

## Why this project

Review operations are usually measured by vibes: a manager reads a few dozen flagged cases and pronounces the classifier fine. This repo shows the measurement done properly at the scale a real team can afford — a fixed labeling budget, spent by a design whose estimator is unbiased, whose bad-segment detection is a pre-declared rule rather than an eyeball, and whose failure mode under a lying risk score is quantified next to the knob that caps it.
